Silent Procedural Defects in Financial Tool Use:

An Evaluation Framework, Offline Validation, and a Live Stress Test

Jasleen Sandhu
▤ Paper ◈ Code ✉ Citation

Abstract

A financial answer can be numerically correct and still break an evidence requirement. I call this a silent procedural defect and build a synthetic benchmark that scores answer correctness separately from retrieval and citation compliance. The questions ask for figures as originally reported, but the data tool returns restated figures by default. The primary measure is the silent-defect rate among outcome-correct runs, reported beside overall correctness. A deterministic checker is validated on planted defects and harmless reorderings, and three scripted policies show that the pipeline recovers behaviour I built in; they say nothing about language models. I then ran one model, gpt-4o-mini, on 100 cases under six conditions, in two repetitions of 1,200 runs. No silent defect occurred in any single-setback condition, and the combined condition produced one in each repetition. A second study lengthened the task to 2, 8 and 16 required retrievals (1,080 runs): with failing tools, 14 of 65 correct runs (21.5%) were silent at 16 retrievals, each citing a figure whose retrieval had failed. Outcome-only evaluation would have scored every one of these runs as a success. These results come from one small model on a synthetic task and do not show how often silent defects occur in financial agents generally.

What does this study measure?

A financial question can specify more than a number: also the reporting basis and the evidence behind it. Ask for operating margin as originally reported, and a tool that returns restated figures by default can still give the right number when nothing was restated. An answer-only check accepts it. By “silent” I mean undetected by outcome-only evaluation; the breach is still visible in a complete tool trace. A defect here is a broken rule, not proven financial loss.

How does it work?

The study has three parts: (1) an offline validation where three scripted policies check that the pipeline recovers behaviours we constructed; (2) a live evaluation of one served model, gpt-4o-mini, on 100 cases under six conditions; and (3) a task-length ladder of 2, 8 and 16 required retrievals. Only parts 2 and 3 say anything about a language model.

Cases and the rubric

The environment is synthetic, so records, required evidence and reference answers are known exactly. The agent has four tools: get_company_profile, get_financials, get_restatement_notes and submit_answer. The financial-data tool returns the restated basis by default, as its description states, while every question asks for figures as originally reported, so an agent only has to accept the default to fall into the trap. Questions cover operating margin, net margin and revenue growth.

Cases fall into two strata. In stratum A no year the question needs was restated, so a wrong-basis retrieval changes nothing numerically and any resulting defect is silent by construction. Stratum B needs a restated year with a gap of at least 0.5 percentage points between the two bases, so a wrong basis gives a wrong answer. The strata are balanced by design (offline: 40 cases, 20 per stratum; live: 100 cases, 50 per stratum), so a pooled rate depends on the chosen mix and not on how often restatements occur in practice.

For reference answer y and submitted value ŷ, correctness is an indicator with a tolerance in percentage points, and it contains no process control. Let R be the required evidence set, G the items successfully retrieved and E the items cited. The checker applies three rules, and a run is defective if any one of them fires.

A = 1{ |ŷ − y| ≤ 0.1 }

The rubric is deliberately narrow. It covers retrieval and citation of evidence because those can be checked deterministically, and it does not cover authorisation, escalation or whether the agent reasoned from the wrong evidence.

Two axes, one estimand. Every run is placed on two independent axes. The primary quantity is the share of outcome-correct runs that are defective, reported next to the overall correctness rate and the unconditional silent-defect rate so the denominator is always visible.

The estimand

Let A be outcome correctness and V a material procedural defect. The primary quantity is the defect rate conditional on a correct outcome, estimated as a pooled ratio over cases i and repeated runs j. Because it is a ratio, correctness sits in the denominator, so it is always reported with the unconditional rate.

σ = P(V=1 | A=1) = E[A·V] / E[A]
σ̂ = Σᵢⱼ Aᵢⱼ Vᵢⱼ / Σᵢⱼ Aᵢⱼ
u = P(A=1, V=1) = σ · aa = P(A=1) is correctness; u is the unconditional silent-defect rate

Estimating the rate

Cases, not runs, are the independent units, because repeated runs of a case share its records. Intervals come from a percentile cluster bootstrap that resamples whole units (10,000 replicates). The offline controls resample cases. The live written plan also specified cases, but because the 100 cases share only 14 companies I resampled companies instead, in line with clustering at the highest shared source group. This change was made after the data were seen, so it is post hoc.

With 14 units the bootstrap is coarse and degenerate when a rate has zero or one events, so I also report two exact upper bounds. Let C be the cases containing a correct run, K the runs per case, Nc the correct runs, and pup the exact one-sided 95% binomial upper bound for the cases containing a silent run. For companies, G is the number with a correct run, gup the exact bound for those containing a silent run, and m the most correct runs in any one company.

case-level: σ ≤ K · pup · C / Nc
company-level: σ ≤ gup · G · m / Ncthe case-level bound treats cases as independent; the company-level bound lets every correct run of an affected company be silent, so it is a conservative envelope, not an estimate

Checking the process

A deterministic checker applies the three rules above. Correctness comes from a separate answer oracle that does not contain these controls. I test the checker on 29 hand-built traces, whose expected labels were written separately from the checker but in the same session, and on eight edits to real live traces made after the data were seen (four planting a defect, four harmless, none changing the submitted value). These tests check the implementation against the stated rubric, not the rubric itself. An independent re-scoring from the raw logs also found four runs whose error was exactly 0.1 points scored incorrect by a floating-point comparison. I corrected this and report corrected counts; no defect count changed.

The live runs

I ran one served model, gpt-4o-mini, at temperature 1.0 with at most twelve tool-calling turns. Six conditions were applied to the same 100 cases, each changing only the environment or the prompt so that answers, required evidence and the rubric stay identical. In flaky each data call fails with probability 0.25; in pressure the prompt asks for as few billed calls as possible; in noisy the question is buried in irrelevant business detail; in distractor an extra tool returns analyst estimates that are not filed figures. combined applies all four and baseline none. Each case ran twice per condition (1,200 runs), and the suite was run twice; the first repetition is primary and the second is reported separately, not pooled. The confirmatory hypothesis was that σ in each non-baseline condition exceeds σ at baseline. The written plan was not independently registered.

The task-length ladder repeats the idea on longer tasks with the same model and rubric: short (a single-year question, 2 required retrievals), medium (the average of four yearly margins, 8) and long (the average of eight, 16). It uses a regenerated world of 60 companies, 60 cases per tier and two runs per case under baseline, flaky and combined (1,080 runs), and its single confirmatory hypothesis was that σ rises with task length. Each ladder case uses one company, so companies are the independent units.

Results

Offline controls (scripted policies, not language-model findings). All 600 scripted runs were complete (three policies, 40 cases, five repetitions each). In stratum A the careful policy produced 100 correct runs and no silent defects, and the default-trap policy produced 100 correct runs, all silently defective. The mixed-error policy produced 95 correct runs, 62 of them silent (σ̂ = 65.3%, 95% CI 55.9–74.2). In stratum B the careful policy again had none, and the default-trap policy produced no correct run, so its rate is undefined, not zero. The mixed-error policy produced 41 correct runs, six silent (σ̂ = 14.6%, 2.5–29.0). The pooled rate for the mixed-error policy (50.0%, 39.1–59.6) depends on the chosen stratum mix. The checker agreed with all 29 hand-built traces and produced the expected flags on the edited live traces (8,962 and 8,950 in the two repetitions). The expectations were written by me, so this is not a blind human audit.

Live evaluation of gpt-4o-mini. All 1,200 runs of the primary repetition were complete; in the second, one combined-condition run without a valid submission was excluded. At baseline, accuracy was 94.5% and no run broke the rubric, so σ̂ was 0 of 189 correct runs (0 of 190 in the second repetition). The baseline errors were all process-compliant revenue-growth questions, consistent with arithmetic error and not procedural failure.

No single-setback condition produced a silent defect in either repetition, among 945 and 944 correct runs. The case-level bound was about 3%, but it treats the 100 cases as independent although they draw on 14 companies, and the company-level bound of 31–32% means the data cannot exclude much larger rates tied to a company-level mechanism. Failed calls alone did not degrade outcomes: under flaky, 19–20% of calls failed and calls per run rose from about 3.0 to 4.4, yet accuracy stayed near 94%. Silent defects occurred only in combined, once in each repetition (σ̂ = 0.5%; bounds 5.0% and 48.6–49.1%). Eight runs were defective in total, two of them silent. Every one fetched the tool’s restated default after failed calls, and seven followed five or more failed calls. The two silent defects were in stratum A; the six loud ones were in stratum B and gave wrong answers. None of the five confirmatory contrasts was supported (Holm-adjusted p = 1.0 throughout).

The confirmatory contrasts supported the hypothesis for both failure conditions: the long-minus-short difference in σ was +21.5 points (11.5–32.1, Holm-adjusted p < 0.001) for flaky and +9.8 (2.8–18.2, p = 0.009) for combined, and zero at baseline. The company-level bounds in these cells were 100% and 90%, so the data fix the direction of the effect more firmly than its size. The unconditional defect rate rises the same way, from 1 to 7 to 56 defective runs of about 120 under flaky, so the rise is not an artefact of conditioning on correctness.

In all 19 silent long-tier runs the model cited at least one required figure it never retrieved, every attempt to fetch it having returned an error; none fetched the figure on the restated basis instead. In a post hoc tabulation, runs with an unretrieved required figure were 0.8% of short runs, 5.8–7.5% of medium runs and 46.7–53.3% of long runs under the two failure conditions, so task length appears to act mainly by making unrecovered failures common. These runs probably stayed correct because one misestimated yearly margin is diluted in an eight-year average and falls inside the 0.1-point tolerance; I did not test this. A post hoc simulation suggests the company-resampling intervals are 6 to 9 points wide even when the true rate is 3%, so the design cannot separate a few percent from zero.

Discussion

The scripted controls verify the construction of the benchmark: in stratum A the default-trap policy reached the right answer without retrieving the requested basis, while in stratum B the same choice changed the answer. They are not evidence about how often an independently developed agent would make that choice. They also show why the denominator matters, since a policy with no correct answers has no estimable σ even if every run breaks the rule.

The live results add a qualified observation. Tool failures, time pressure, a buried request or a distractor tool on their own produced no silent defect; defects appeared when a long task met failing tools, and then the model cited figures it had never retrieved. Two silent defects in 2,263 correct runs in the first suite make that a hypothesis for a larger study and not an estimate, and because the cases share only 14 companies, the absence of defects elsewhere should not be read as evidence of safety. The near-zero baseline rate has three readings the data cannot fully separate: the model may be reliable here, the checker may miss defects, or the task may be too easy. The ladder speaks to the third reading for one model; it does not address the checker or other models. Stratified samples of 76 traces from the first suite and 40 from the long tier have been drawn for a blind audit, but none has yet been labelled by an independent person.

Process failures among outcome-correct runs have been reported at much higher rates elsewhere, but a rate belongs to a model together with a rubric, benchmark and task. My rubric covers only three retrieval and citation rules, so a low rate here is expected even for an agent that would fail broader controls; I have not scored the same traces under a broader rubric and cannot say how much of the gap it explains.

Limitations and future work

The scripted policies stand in for a live model, so the offline validation does not measure language-model reliability. The live evaluation covers one small model, one synthetic world in which baseline accuracy was already high, six conditions of my choosing and two repetitions on the same 100 cases, which are not independent samples. The written plans were not independently registered, the sample sizes rested on a heuristic and not on simulation, and no random sample of live traces has been audited independently. The cases share only 14 companies, and the task involves no ledger writes or authorisation checks, so it cannot speak to the accounts-receivable controls that motivate the framework. The correctness tolerance may also let a misestimated figure pass in a long average.

The first next step is the blind human audit of real traces. After that, the live evaluation should be extended to several models and a harder world, with a protocol frozen and time-stamped before collection and case and repetition counts chosen by simulation. The proposed accounts-receivable extension would add payment records, invoice matching and ledger commitments, and would need its own outcome oracle and evidence-timing predicates. A pre-commit evidence gate, in the form of a verify-and-retry loop, may lower the silent-defect rate there, but this is a conjecture and not a result. It could be tested only where the baseline produces defects, such as the long tier with failing tools, and it should check requirements the scoring rubric does not rely on exclusively, since a gate that applies the same checks as the scorer would lower measured defects by construction. None of these extensions is measured here.

Related Work

Agent benchmarks such as τ-bench (Yao et al., 2024) check final states under policy guidelines. The closest work separates outcome from process in the same way: CXBench (Yu et al., 2026) reports that false passes, which pass the outcome check but fail the process check, are 80.07% of outcome passes in regulated customer-service tasks, and Cao et al. (2026) report that 27% to 78% of benchmark-reported successes in τ-bench are corrupt for three models. Uesato et al. (2022) report error among correct final answers falling from 14.0% to 3.4% for the best process-based results in mathematics, a conditional measurement close in form to σ. I apply the same conditional framing to observable tool calls and citations in a finance setting, with an explicit denominator and resampling at the level of shared source records. My rubric is narrower than theirs, so my rates should not be compared directly with theirs.
